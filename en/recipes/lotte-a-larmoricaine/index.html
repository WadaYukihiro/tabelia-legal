Method

125 min

Trim the monkfish and prepare the shells, tomatoes and aromatics

  • 1 Check that the 6 raw whole langoustines have thawed in the refrigerator and have no ice remaining before removing their heads and shells.
  • 2 Remove any remaining skin or thin gray membrane from the monkfish with a knife. Cut into even 3–4cm bone-in pieces and pat dry.
  • 3 Remove the heads and body shells from 6 langoustines, briefly rinse the heads and shells and pat dry.
  • 4 Blanch and peel 500g tomatoes, remove the seeds and chop roughly.
  • 5 Mince 4 shallots and crush 1 garlic clove. Chop the parsley and tarragon leaves, reserving the parsley stems.
  • 6 Peel a strip of orange skin 2cm wide and 6cm long and remove the white pith with a knife.

Ready when

Continue when the langoustines are fully thawed and the monkfish has no skin or membrane remaining and a dry surface.

Caution

The membrane is edible but has little flavor and leaves an unpleasant texture in the mouth.

27 min

Sauté the shells and monkfish in olive oil, then add shallots

  • 1 Heat 100 ml olive oil over medium heat in a heavy pot 28 cm across. Add the langoustine heads and shells and as much monkfish as fits in 1 layer; cook for 4 minutes, turning occasionally.
  • 2 If there is a second batch of monkfish, lift the first batch onto a plate, leaving the heads, shells and oil in the pot. Cook the remaining fish in the same pot for 4 minutes, then return the first batch and any juices to the pot.
  • 3 Add the shallots and cook for 2 minutes.
  • 4 MeanwhileWhile sautéing, start bringing 2L water to a boil for the rice.

Ready when

Continue when the fish surface is white and lightly browned at the edges, the shells are red and the shallots are lightly colored.

320 min

Add Cognac, wine, tomatoes and herbs and simmer covered for 15 minutes

  • 1 Dissolve 15g tomato paste in 375ml white wine.
  • 2 Take the pot off the heat before pouring in 30ml Cognac and the wine, then return to medium heat.
  • 3 Add the garlic, orange peel, tarragon, chopped parsley, parsley stems, tomatoes, 1 bay leaf, 2 thyme sprigs, 8g salt, 0.5g white pepper and 0.3g cayenne pepper.
  • 4 When boiling, cover, reduce to low heat and simmer for 15 minutes.
  • 5 Check that the thickest fish piece is white and opaque all the way to the bone.
  • 6 MeanwhileWhile simmering, wash 240g basmati rice. Add 20g salt to the 2L boiling water, cook the rice for 10 minutes, drain in a strainer and cover to keep warm.

Ready when

Continue when the flesh is white and opaque to the center but still slightly springy when pressed.

Caution

Pouring Cognac into a pot still over the heat can cause flames.

412 min

Remove the fish, strain the sauce and add cream

  • 1 Transfer the monkfish to a warmed plate, cover with foil and keep warm.
  • 2 Remove the bay leaf, thyme, parsley stems and orange peel. Pass the cooking liquid and shells through a fine strainer, pressing with the back of a ladle.
  • 3 Return the strained sauce to the pot and reduce over high heat to about 400ml, then mix in 50ml cream and adjust the seasoning with salt.

Ready when

Continue when the sauce is smooth and orange-colored and lightly coats the back of a spoon.

53 min

Plate the fish with sauce and a ring of rice

  • 1 Arrange the basmati rice in a ring around the edge of warmed plates and put the monkfish in the center.
  • 2 Pour the sauce over the monkfish.

Ready when

The dish is ready when the fish is covered with sauce and surrounded by a ring of rice.

About this dish

At the fish auction in the Breton port of Le Guilvinec, monkfish arrive as tails with their enormous heads removed. In France the fish is sold by the tail, its lean, small-bone-free flesh cooked by searing or braising much like meat. In 2024, all 7 French auction ports with the highest monkfish trading volumes were in Brittany.

Whether the sauce should be called américaine, “American-style,” or armoricaine, after Armorica, the old name for Brittany, has long been debated in France. The américaine account connects it with a lobster dish made around 1860 by Pierre Fraysse, known as Peters, a cook who had returned from working in America. That account also holds that the dish has nothing specifically Breton about it.

Chef Authen’s tip

The lotte in this dish is marine monkfish. In Escoffier’s cookbook, however, the fish called lotte appears among freshwater fish. Its flesh is described as ordinary and its liver as the valuable part; it is thought to refer to burbot.
