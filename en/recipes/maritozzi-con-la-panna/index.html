Maritozzi con la PannaMaritozzi with Whipped Cream

Difficulty★★★☆☆ Prep1 hr Cook15 min Rest2 hr 15 min servings8

A Roman breakfast pastry of fluffy brioche filled generously with cream. Its name comes from marito, “husband,” recalling its former role as a gift to a fiancée. Sweet yeast buns fragrant with grated orange zest and honey surround mouthfuls of cream: a weekend indulgence of which Rome is proud.

Method

110 min

Activate the yeast and start the dough

  • 1 Dissolve 15g fresh yeast in 100ml lukewarm milk, add 1 tsp sugar and leave for 5 minutes.
  • 2 Grate only the outer orange zest.
  • 3 Combine 300g strong flour and 70g sugar in a bowl.
  • 4 Add the yeast mixture, 2 eggs, 20g honey and grated orange zest, and begin kneading.

Ready when

Bubbles on the yeast mixture indicate activation. Proceed when the dough comes together.

Caution

Milk that is too hot kills the yeast. Keep it at 35–38°C.

215 min

Add butter and knead

  • 1 Add 70g room-temperature butter in 3–4 additions, kneading well after each.
  • 2 Add 5g salt and knead for another 8–10 minutes.

Ready when

Proceed when a piece of dough stretches into a thin membrane.

Caution

Do not add all the butter at once. Add gradually while developing the gluten.

31 hr 30 min

First rise

  • 1 Place the dough in a bowl and cover with plastic wrap.
  • 2 Let rise in a warm place at 28–30°C for about 90 minutes.

Ready when

After about 90 minutes, proceed when it reaches 2 times its original volume.

415 min

Divide and shape

  • 1 Turn onto a floured surface and gently deflate.
  • 2 Divide into 8 portions, about 80g per 1 piece, and round them, tightening the surface.
  • 3 Shape into small, rounded buns, making them slightly oval if necessary.
  • 4 Arrange spaced apart on a baking tray lined with parchment and cover with plastic wrap.

Ready when

Proceed when the surfaces are smooth and taut and the buns have uniform rounded shapes.

545 min

Second rise

  • 1 (During this time) Preheat the oven to 190°C.
  • 2 Let rise a second time for 45 minutes.
  • 3 MeanwhileDuring the second rise, preheat the oven to 190°C.

Ready when

After about 45 minutes, proceed when the dough has expanded to around 1.5 times its size.

620 min

Bake and glaze

  • 1 Put 30ml water and 20g sugar in a small saucepan over medium heat to make the glaze.
  • 2 Bake at 190°C for 12–14 minutes.
  • 3 Brush the glaze all over while the buns are hot.
  • 4 Cool completely on a wire rack.

Ready when

They are baked when the surfaces are golden brown.

75 min

Whip the cream

  • 1 Put 300ml cream and 20g icing sugar in a chilled bowl.
  • 2 Whip with a hand mixer to medium peaks.

Ready when

Proceed when a spoonful holds its shape without flowing and only the tips of the peaks bend slightly.

Caution

Overwhipping encourages separation. Stop before the cream becomes grainy.

810 min

Slit and fill with cream

  • 1 Cut a deep slit into the center of the top of each completely cooled maritozzo.
  • 2 Generously pack the opening with whipped cream.
  • 3 Use a palette knife to smooth the cream flat along the top of the bun.
  • 4 Finish just before serving.

Ready when

The buns are ready when the openings are full and a broad white surface of cream is visible.

Caution

The dough absorbs moisture from the cream over time. Serve immediately after filling.

About this dish

The name maritozzo derives from marito, meaning “husband.” Young Romans once offered these sweet yeast buns to their fiancées as tokens of courtship, sometimes hiding a gold ring or small gift inside. Finding the ring when she broke the bun conveyed the proposal of marriage. The ring has disappeared from the modern maritozzo, leaving generous cream in its place.

Rome long had a custom of eating maritozzi for Friday breakfast. Sweet bread was an exception even on fasting days during Lent, or Quaresima, and these small yeast buns comforted the city’s people during the fast. Market workers and early-rising craftspeople taking a maritozzo with coffee at a bar remain part of the Roman morning.

Essential to Rome’s breakfast culture, they have long been a staple of bar display cases. A worldwide craze arose in the early 2020s, bringing specialist shops to Japan too. Yet the original maritozzo is freshly baked bread filled with cream and eaten immediately. As time passes, the dough absorbs the cream’s moisture and loses its intended texture.

Chef Authen’s tip

During Japan’s maritozzo craze, shops competed over cream varieties: matcha, cheese and fruit fillings abounded, while the essential bread was often commercial bread or an ordinary roll. Roman bakeries take the opposite approach. The cream is simply lightly sweetened fresh cream and does not change. Bakers devote their skill to the bread: its second rise, honey and citrus zest, and syrup after baking. The cream exists to help savor this bread. Something ends the moment the pedestal becomes the protagonist.
