Tortelli Maremmani al RagùMaremman Tortelli with Meat Ragù

Difficulty★★★★☆ Prep1 hr 40 min Cook4 hr 10 min Rest12 hr servings4

A festive primo from Maremma in southern Tuscany. A filling of sheep’s-milk ricotta, spinach and sheep’s-milk pecorino is enclosed in thin, egg-only handmade sfoglia and shaped into large squares. A rich ragù of ground beef and pork, red wine and tomato simmers for nearly 3 hours. Even hand-rolling, moisture control in the filling and gentle boiling without tearing determine the result.

Ingredients (4 servings)

  • Soft to all-purpose Italian type 00 flour300 gFor sfoglia; sift before use
  • Whole eggs3 piecesAbout 55 g each; for the dough. Keep refrigerated until just before use
  • Fine durum wheat semolina40 gFor dusting and beneath shaped pasta
  • Sheep’s-milk ricotta300 gDrain overnight in a sieve in the refrigerator
  • Spinach500 gRemove stems and wash well; about 150 g after boiling and squeezing firmly
  • Grated aged Pecorino Toscano80 gFinely grate for filling: use 70 g, adding up to 10 g only if loose
  • Egg yolk1 pieceTo bind the filling
  • Nutmeg0.5 gGrate fresh from a whole nutmeg
  • Coarsely ground beef chuck250 gFor ragù; coarsely grind or finely chop with a knife
  • Coarsely ground pork shoulder150 gProvides the ragù’s fat and richness
  • Onion100 gDice 3 mm for the soffritto
  • Carrot80 gDice 3 mm for the soffritto
  • Celery60 gRemove strings and dice 3 mm for the soffritto
  • Extra virgin olive oil50 mlFor soffritto and browning the meat
  • Dry red wine120 mlDry, predominantly Sangiovese; provides acidity and structure
  • Tomato passata400 gThe ragù base
  • Concentrated tomato paste15 gCook with the soffritto to develop sweetness
  • Unsalted meat broth500 mlAdd gradually during simmering; use warm
  • Bay leaf1 leafAdd during simmering and remove at the finish
  • Garlic1 clovePeel and crush with the flat of a knife; add to soffritto
  • Coarse salt for pasta water40 gFor 4 L water at 10 g/L
  • Fine salt13 g3 g for dough, 4 g for filling, 5 g for simmering ragù and 1 g for final seasoning
  • Black peppercorns2 gGrind as needed, dividing among filling, simmering ragù, final seasoning and plating
  • Grated Pecorino Toscano for finishing40 gSprinkle over each plate

Method

112 hr

Drain the sheep’s-milk ricotta the day before

  • 1 Place 300g sheep’s-milk ricotta in a sieve over a bowl, cover with plastic wrap, and drain in the refrigerator for 12 hours.

Ready when

There should be 30–50ml whey in the bowl, and no water should seep from the ricotta when pressed with a finger.

Caution

Insufficient draining makes the filling loose, wetting and tearing the dough during shaping.

Tips

Sheep’s-milk ricotta has a stronger sweet dairy aroma than cow’s-milk ricotta and forms the foundation of the Maremman filling.

235 min

Make the soffritto and brown the meat

  • 1 Meanwhile(While browning) Warm 500ml meat broth in another pot and keep warm over low heat until the braise is finished.
  • 2 Put 50ml olive oil, 1 crushed garlic clove, and 100g onion, 80g carrot, and 60g celery cut into 3mm dice in a heavy pot. Cook over medium-low heat for 15–18 minutes without browning.
  • 3 Add 15g tomato paste and cook over low heat for 2 minutes. Add 250g coarsely ground beef chuck and 150g coarsely ground pork shoulder, and brown over medium-high heat for 10–12 minutes, breaking up the meat.

Ready when

The vegetables should be soft and sweet-smelling, with a thin browned film, or fond, on the bottom.

All moisture released by the meat should have evaporated; the meat should be brown and the fat clear.

Caution

Rushing the soffritto, the base of slowly cooked chopped aromatic vegetables, over high heat burns the onion and makes the whole ragù bitter.

Moving the meat too much immediately after adding it releases water, making it boil rather than brown.

Tips

Coarsely ground or hand-chopped meat retains distinct pieces after simmering.

315 min

Add red wine and tomato to start the braise

  • 1 Pour in 120ml red wine and boil over medium-high heat for 4–5 minutes, scraping the bottom.
  • 2 Add 200ml of the broth, 400g passata, 1 bay leaf, 5g salt, and 0.5g black pepper. Reduce to low heat and set the lid 7/10 of the way ajar.

Ready when

The sharp acidic smell of wine should be gone and the surface should settle to a bubble every few seconds.

Caution

Never ignite the alcohol. Boil to drive it off.

Continued vigorous boiling tightens the meat and makes the ragù dry.

Tips

A dry Sangiovese-based red is a local Maremman wine whose tannins give structure to the ragù.

42 hr 30 min

Simmer the ragù for 2 hours 30 minutes

  • 1 Meanwhile(While simmering) Continue with steps 5–10.
  • 2 Simmer for 2 hours 30 minutes, scraping the bottom every 20–30 minutes. If the meat becomes exposed, add the warm broth in 100ml increments, using only as much of the remaining 300ml as needed to keep the bottom from drying out.

Ready when

Red fat should rise to the surface and a wooden spatula drawn across the bottom should leave a trail for 2–3 seconds.

The meat should separate into pieces tender enough to crush with the tongue.

Caution

Adding a large quantity of broth at once lowers the temperature and encourages fat and liquid to separate.

Closing the lid fully prevents evaporation and leaves a loose ragù.

520 min

Knead the egg-only sfoglia

  • 1 Make a well in a mound of 300g type 00 flour, add 3 whole eggs and 3g salt, and beat with a fork while incorporating flour from the inner edge.
  • 2 Once it comes together, push with the heel of your hand and fold back repeatedly, kneading for 10–12 minutes.

Ready when

The surface should be silky smooth and elastic enough for a finger indentation to return slowly.

No large air pockets should be visible in a cut cross-section.

Caution

Collapsing all the flour into the eggs at once lets them spill, making a mess and altering the proportions.

Even if dry and crumbly, do not pour in water: wet your hands to add only a few drops of moisture and continue kneading.

Tips

Traditional Tuscan sfoglia is kneaded with eggs alone, without oil or water, making dough that stays strong even when rolled thin.

630 min

Rest the dough

  • 1 Meanwhile(While the dough rests) Start bringing 2L water to a boil.
  • 2 Wrap the dough tightly in plastic wrap without gaps. Rest in the refrigerator for 30 minutes and keep refrigerated until just before rolling.

Ready when

The dough should relax enough that a light indentation returns slowly only halfway.

Caution

Gaps in the wrap let the surface dry into a hard skin that tears during rolling.

Tips

Resting relaxes the gluten so the dough can be rolled thinly and evenly with a rolling pin.

715 min

Boil and dry the spinach

  • 1 Remove the stems from 500g spinach, wash, and boil in the boiling water for 2 minutes.
  • 2 Transfer to cold water, wrap in a cloth, and squeeze firmly. Chop into 1–2mm widths.

Ready when

The squeezed spinach should weigh about 150g and release no drops when squeezed.

Caution

Poor draining makes the filling loose and causes the dough to tear during boiling.

Coarsely chopped leaf fibers can pierce the dough during shaping.

Tips

Do not salt the water: the ricotta and pecorino already add salt to the filling, so salting here would make the whole dish too salty.

815 min

Make the ricotta and spinach filling

  • 1 Meanwhile(While the filling chills) Roll the dough in step 9.
  • 2 Work the ricotta until smooth. Evenly mix in the spinach, 70g pecorino, 1 egg yolk, 0.5g nutmeg, 4g salt, and 0.5g black pepper. Press plastic wrap onto the surface and refrigerate for 30 minutes to firm.

Ready when

The filling should hold its shape when a spoonful is tilted and maintain a peak.

Caution

Overmixing separates the ricotta fat and makes the filling watery.

If loose, do not add flour: add 10g pecorino to firm it.

Tips

Freshly grating nutmeg releases its volatile aroma and brings together the sheep’s-milk flavor.

930 min

Roll out the sfoglia by hand

  • 1 Divide the dough into 4 portions. Wrap and refrigerate the unused portions. On a work surface dusted with 5g semolina, roll one portion around the rolling pin, turning by 90 degrees as you work. Roll into a rectangle 1mm thick, aiming for 21cm wide and 42cm long.

Ready when

The outline of a palm should show through, and the thickness should be even throughout.

Caution

Too much semolina dries the edges and prevents sealing.

Spending too long on a sheet dries it out: cover the rolled sheet with a cloth until shaping.

Tips

Wrapping the dough around the rolling pin is the Tuscan approach, giving a rougher surface than a machine that holds sauce well.

1035 min

Shape large square tortelli

  • 1 Divide the filling into 4 portions. On the near half of one sheet, distribute 1/4 of the filling among 9 mounds in 3 rows and 3 columns, spaced 7cm apart. Position the centers of the outer mounds 3.5cm from the sheet edges and the fold line. Lightly moisten around the filling, fold the far half toward you, expel the air, and seal.
  • 2 Cut into 7cm squares with a pastry wheel and press the edges shut. Arrange separately on a tray dusted with semolina. Whenever a tray is full, cover and refrigerate it.
  • 3 Roll and shape the remaining dough the same way to make about 36 tortelli (9 per person).

Ready when

Every tortello should be fully sealed, and the filling should not shift when lifted by hand.

The arranged pieces should not touch each other.

Caution

Trapped air expands during boiling and makes them burst.

Too much water dissolves the dough and makes the edges slip.

Tips

Maremman tortelli are large, 7–8cm per side, and 9 per person is a standard serving.

1115 min

Finish the ragù and prepare the cooking water

  • 1 Meanwhile(While the water heats) Warm 4 plates with hot water.
  • 2 Remove the bay leaf from the ragù, season with 1g salt and 0.5g black pepper, and keep warm over very low heat.
  • 3 Bring 4L water to a boil in a large pot and add 40g coarse salt.

Ready when

The ragù should be warm and the water gently boiling.

Caution

Reheating the ragù over high heat evaporates too much liquid and makes it difficult to coat the pasta.

Tips

Salt the cooking water at 10g/L. Since the filling and ragù contain salt, do not make it stronger.

1224 min

Boil the tortelli

  • 1 Take 9 tortelli at a time from the refrigerator and cook in gently boiling water, using 3 minutes after they float as a guide. Measure the center of the filling in the largest piece with a food thermometer. Once the center has remained at or above 75°C for 1 minute, lift out with a slotted ladle.
  • 2 Spread a thin layer of ragù on a warmed plate, place the cooked tortelli on it, and spoon more ragù over them. Cook the remaining batches the same way, finishing each plate in turn. Add one plate’s share of cheese and pepper from step 13 and serve immediately.

Ready when

The dough and filling should be cooked through, and the center of the filling must have remained at or above 75°C for 1 minute.

Caution

A vigorous boil makes the pieces collide and tear. Cook at a gentle boil.

Crowding the pot lowers the temperature, causing uneven cooking and tearing.

138 min

Layer with ragù and plate

  • 1 Distribute the ragù over the 9 tortelli on each plate. Sprinkle each with 10g finishing pecorino and 0.125g black pepper (40g and 0.5g in total across 4 plates).

Ready when

Every plate’s tortelli should have ragù and cheese distributed over them.

Caution

Do not stack the cooked tortelli in a frying pan or stir them with tongs.

Tips

In Maremma, ragù is poured over to show the meat rather than mixing all of it through.

Southern Tuscany is olive-oil country and does not finish with butter. Cooking water, the ragù’s fat, and pecorino bind the coating.

About this dish

Maremma is an area of plains and hills facing the Tyrrhenian Sea in southern Tuscany. Once a rough, marshy land, it developed a culture of cattle herders known as butteri.

Its tortelli are notably larger than those of other regions, measuring 7–8 cm per side. A dish for holidays and Sunday meals, they have long been served with a slowly simmered beef and pork ragù. Both ricotta and pecorino in the filling are made from sheep’s milk, ingredients supported by Maremma’s sheep farming.

In trattorias around Grosseto, families still gather around this dish on Sunday afternoons. Local practice is to pour some ragù over to display the meat, rather than mixing all of it through.

Chef Authen’s tip

Southern Tuscany is olive-oil country and does not use butter to finish. With similar filled pasta, Emilia to the north coats it in butter, while here the ragù’s fat, cooking water and pecorino perform that role. Crossing a single regional boundary changes the fat that brings the dish together.
