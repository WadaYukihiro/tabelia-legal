Risotto al Nero di Seppia alla VenezianaVenetian Cuttlefish Ink Risotto

Difficulty★★★★☆ Prep1 hr 3 min Cook1 hr 44 min servings4

A Venetian primo made by cleaning whole cuttlefish, removing their ink sacs yourself, and cooking rice with that ink and a fumetto drawn from fish bones. As in seppie in nero, the ink first simmers with the cuttlefish for 20 minutes to soften its harshness and penetrate the flesh; the remainder is added late in the risotto to deepen the color. Vialone Nano rice is toasted with white wine and cooked for 16 minutes with additions of hot stock until all’onda: flowing in waves, with soft outer grains and a slight core. Cuttlefish are divided between braising and finishing; the finishing portion is briefly seared separately and added at the end. Final mantecatura uses cold butter only, with no cheese, following the seafood-risotto rule.

Method

135 min

Clean the cuttlefish and remove the ink sacs

  • 1 Rinse 1400 g whole cuttlefish under running water, insert your fingers into the body to detach the connection to the arms, then gently pull out the arms with the innards.
  • 2 Find the elongated silver to blue-black ink sac among the innards with your fingertips. Cut it free at the base without breaking it, place on a small plate and refrigerate.
  • 3 Remove the translucent bone (cartilage) and inner membrane from the body, then lift the outer skin from an edge with your thumb and peel it off completely.
  • 4 Push out and remove the beak at the center of the arms, cut away the eyes and rub off the hard sucker rings with your fingers.
  • 5 Rinse body and arms 2 times in a bowl of cold water to remove sand and remaining ink, then pat completely dry with paper towels.
  • 6 Weigh the cleaned edible flesh, about 800 g; cut bodies into 1 cm cubes and arms into 2 cm lengths.
  • 7 Weigh 550 g for braising and 250 g for finishing into separate bowls and refrigerate both.

Ready when

All ink sacs are intact on their separate plate.

About 800 g edible flesh is divided into 2 bowls, 550 g for braising and 250 g for finishing.

No droplets remain on the flesh surface.

Caution

Ink sacs are thin and fragile. Pulling the innards hard can burst them inside the body, blackening the flesh and preventing recovery of the ink.

A retained beak is a hard foreign object when bitten.

Sautéing wet flesh lowers the temperature, makes the cuttlefish release moisture and toughens it.

Tips

Cuttlefish yield about 55–60% after removing innards, bone, skin and eyes. If 1400 g whole cuttlefish yields less than 800 g, divide braising and finishing portions in a 2:1 ratio.

Venetian fishmongers normally sell cuttlefish with ink sacs and will wrap the sacs separately if asked.

220 min

Prepare the fumetto

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile the bones soak, roughly chop 80 g white onion, cut 60 g carrot into 2 cm rounds, and remove strings from 60 g celery and cut into 3 cm lengths.
  • 2 Completely remove gills and blood from 800 g white-fish bones and heads using fingers and a knife tip, then soak in cold water for 15 minutes.
  • 3 Rinse the soaked bones under running water, drain and put in a heavy saucepan.
  • 4 Add the prepared 80 g white onion, 60 g carrot and 60 g celery.
  • 5 Tie 5 g parsley stems together, add to the pan with 5 black peppercorns.
  • 6 Pour in 2400 ml cold water and set over medium heat.

Ready when

The bones and aromatics are fully submerged in 2400 ml cold water.

The soaking water is no longer bloody and no dark-red blood remains on the bones.

Caution

Remaining blood causes fishy flavor and cloudiness.

Do not salt the fumetto: it concentrates in the risotto and would become salty.

Tips

In Veneto, white-fish bones such as sea bass, flounder and gurnard are used; oily fish are avoided for their strong fat and odor.

335 min

Simmer, strain and keep the fumetto warm

  • 1 Just before boiling, lower the heat and carefully skim surface scum and foam with a ladle.
  • 2 Maintain a gentle tremble over low heat at 85–90°C and simmer uncovered for 25 minutes.
  • 3 Turn off the heat and gently strain into another saucepan through a fine sieve lined with paper towels, allowing it to drain naturally without pressing the bones.
  • 4 Measure the strained liquid and add hot water if needed to reach 1800 ml.
  • 5 Keep the strained fumetto over low heat at around 80°C throughout.

Ready when

The stock is clear pale amber with a clean aroma and no fishiness.

There are 1800 ml strained, adjusted stock.

Caution

A rolling boil makes the stock cloudy and produces fishiness and bitterness.

Simmering beyond 25 minutes extracts bitterness and harshness from the bones.

A lid returns steam and traps fishy odors.

Tips

Of the 1800 ml, 50 ml dissolves ink, 250 ml braises cuttlefish and 1350 ml cooks rice; the remaining 150 ml is reserve for topping up.

48 min

Dissolve the ink and divide for two uses

  • 1 Take the ink sacs from the refrigerator and place in a tea strainer over a small bowl.
  • 2 Slit them with a knife tip and press with the back of a spoon to strain the contents into the bowl; discard the membranes.
  • 3 Add 8 g packaged cuttlefish ink to the same bowl.
  • 4 Pour in 50 ml hot fumetto and whisk for 30 seconds until fully dissolved, without grains or lumps.
  • 5 Divide the ink liquid into 2/3 for braising and 1/3 for risotto in small bowls, cover both with plastic wrap and place near the stove.

Ready when

The ink is a uniformly thick black liquid without sediment.

It is divided between 2 bowls: 2/3 for braising and 1/3 for risotto.

Caution

Ink stains clothes, wooden boards and grout permanently. Lay newspaper or paper around the bowl.

Undissolved lumps remain as black grains in the risotto and spoil the texture.

Tips

The traditional two-stage use cooks out fishiness and harsh edges in the braise as ink penetrates the cuttlefish, while the risotto portion strengthens final color and gloss.

Natural sacs alone can give a grayish color, so supplementing with packaged ink is common even in Venetian restaurants.

520 min

Make soffritto and combine cuttlefish with ink

  • 1 Very finely chop 120 g white onion.
  • 2 Remove the germs from 2 garlic cloves and crush with the flat of a knife.
  • 3 Put 30 ml olive oil and the crushed garlic in a wide, heavy 28 cm saucepan, which will also finish the risotto, over low heat.
  • 4 Infuse for about 4 minutes until the garlic is lightly colored, then remove and discard it.
  • 5 Add the 120 g chopped onion and sauté over medium-low heat for 8–10 minutes, stirring with a wooden spoon until translucent and melting without browning.
  • 6 Add the 550 g braising cuttlefish and sauté over medium heat for 3 minutes until the surface turns white and contracts.
  • 7 Add 10 g tomato paste and sauté for 1 minute to remove acidity.
  • 8 Add 15 g chopped parsley leaves and the 2/3 braising ink liquid, stirring for 30 seconds until evenly black.
  • 9 Pour in 250 ml hot fumetto, add 4 g salt and bring to a simmer over medium heat.

Ready when

Cuttlefish and onion are evenly black-brown with ink, and the liquid bubbles to the pan edges.

The bottom is not scorched and any graininess of the ink is fully dissolved.

Caution

Browning the onion mixes brown into the black ink and muddies the final color.

Long sautéing over high heat removes moisture and makes cuttlefish rubbery.

Once ink is added it burns easily: do not leave it longer than 30 seconds before adding fumetto.

Tips

Cooking cuttlefish in 2 stages combines tender, long-braised flesh with a firmer portion briefly seared at the end.

620 min

Simmer the cuttlefish for 20 minutes

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile the cuttlefish simmers, cut 40 g cold butter into 1.5 cm cubes and refrigerate until needed.
  • 2 MeanwhileWhile the cuttlefish simmers, start warming 4 flat serving plates to around 50°C.
  • 3 Lower the heat, cover with the lid slightly ajar and simmer for 20 minutes.
  • 4 At about 10 minutes, check the liquid; if it falls below half the height of the cuttlefish, add 50 ml hot fumetto.
  • 5 Turn off the heat after 20 minutes.

Ready when

The cuttlefish feels tender within its elasticity when pressed with a finger.

The liquid has reduced to about half the cuttlefish’s height and is black and thick.

Caution

If the liquid dries out completely, the ink scorches on the base and becomes bitter.

Continuous hard boiling tightens and toughens the cuttlefish. Keep only a gentle surface movement over low heat.

Tips

These 20 minutes relax the fibers and turn the ink’s fishiness and harshness into rounded savor: the essence of seppie in nero.

74 min

Toast the rice

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile toasting, check that the fumetto on the neighboring burner is held at around 80°C.
  • 2 Set the 28 cm pan containing the braised cuttlefish over medium heat and drizzle in 15 ml olive oil.
  • 3 Add 320 g Vialone Nano rice without washing.
  • 4 Stir constantly with a wooden spoon over medium heat for 2–3 minutes, working the grains against the pan and oil.

Ready when

The grain exteriors are translucent, with only the central endosperm remaining white.

The grains are hot and a dry toasted aroma begins to rise.

Caution

Washing removes surface starch and prevents a creamy risotto.

Do not brown the rice: toasted browning aromas interfere with the ink flavor.

Tips

Toasting coats the rice in oil, helping grains hold their shape and retain a slight core during cooking.

83 min

Reduce the white wine

  • 1 Pour in 100 ml room-temperature dry white wine at once and bring to a boil over medium-high heat while stirring.

Ready when

The sharp acidic wine smell disappears, almost no liquid remains on the base, and the sound changes to a sizzle.

Caution

Never ignite alcohol. Boil to evaporate it.

Adding fumetto before reducing the wine leaves an alcoholic smell.

Tips

Veneto cooks use Soave or dry Garganega-based wines, avoiding strongly oaked wines.

916 min

Cook for 16 minutes with additions of fumetto

  • 1 MeanwhileAt about 12 minutes, between stirring the risotto, heat 10 ml olive oil in a separate frying pan over high heat until smoking; spread in the 250 g finishing cuttlefish and sear for 30–40 seconds, whitening only the surface, then turn off the heat.
  • 2 Pour in 150 ml hot fumetto and stir with a wooden spoon along the pan bottom.
  • 3 Whenever liquid reduces to just level with the rice, add 120 ml hot fumetto, 10 times in total: with the initial 150 ml, the total used is 1350 ml.
  • 4 Maintain medium heat and turn the rice broadly from the bottom every 1–2 minutes.
  • 5 At 8 minutes from the start of rice cooking, sprinkle in 4 g salt and stir.
  • 6 At 10 minutes, add all the reserved 1/3 ink liquid for the risotto and stir for 30 seconds until uniformly jet-black.
  • 7 At 14 minutes, take 1 grain and bite it to check the remaining core.
  • 8 Add the 250 g seared finishing cuttlefish with its pan oil and cook, stirring, for the final 2 minutes.

Ready when

A total of 16 minutes has elapsed; grain exteriors are beginning to dissolve, leaving only a very fine core.

Shaking the pan creates waves, and a spoon track closes in about 1 second.

The fumetto used totals 1350 ml.

Caution

Cold fumetto lowers rice temperature and causes uneven cooking: always use it hot, around 80°C.

Too much stirring breaks grains into porridge; too little scorches the bottom.

Do not move the finishing cuttlefish excessively while searing. Released moisture makes it boil rather than sear.

Adding the finishing cuttlefish before 14 minutes toughens it. Keep strictly to the final 2 minutes.

Tips

Veneto’s onda, or wave, means a risotto loose enough to flow slowly when the plate is tilted, markedly wetter than Milanese risotto.

Searing the finishing cuttlefish over high heat rather than adding it raw develops aroma without releasing excess water, preserving fluidity and salt balance.

106 min

Mantecatura and plating

  • 1 Turn the heat fully off and remove the pan from it.
  • 2 Add all 40 g cold butter straight from the refrigerator.
  • 3 Shake the pan strongly from side to side while stirring quickly from the bottom with a wooden spoon for 20–30 seconds to emulsify the butter into the rice.
  • 4 Add 5 g salt and 1/2 teaspoon freshly ground black pepper and stir once.
  • 5 Cover and rest for 1 minute.
  • 6 Uncover, give one broad stir and pour equally onto 4 warmed flat plates.
  • 7 Tap the bottom of each plate lightly with your palm so the risotto spreads naturally flat.
  • 8 Drizzle 15 ml finishing olive oil in thin streams across the 4 plates and scatter with 5 g chopped parsley leaves.

Ready when

A glossy black film forms on the surface, and the risotto spreads gently with wavy edges.

The grains do not separate; the whole is unified and creamy.

Caution

Adding butter over heat separates the oil and makes the surface greasy. Always turn off the heat first.

Do not add Parmigiano or other cheese to seafood risotto: it masks the sea flavor.

Waiting to serve allows residual heat to evaporate moisture and set the rice. Serve immediately after finishing.

Tips

The 1-minute rest settles it into all’onda consistency and is always observed in Veneto kitchens.

Its black color makes a characteristically Venetian contrast on a white flat plate.

About this dish

Venice is built on a lagoon, the laguna. Cuttlefish are plentiful in its shallows and still appear every morning at the fish market beside the Rialto Bridge.

Risotto al nero di seppia uses these cuttlefish with their ink sacs. Flesh and ink flavor the rice together, turning the plate entirely black. Supported by lagoon fishing, it has been called risotto nero or seppie al nero in Venetian dialect.

Venetian trattorias still treat it as a signature dish. No cheese is sprinkled on at the end, reflecting the Veneto custom of not pairing cheese with seafood.

Chef Authen’s tip

Avoiding cheese on seafood pasta and risotto in Italy is often described as a rule. Yet it is not universal across the country; regional exceptions exist. For Venetian black risotto, there is no exception, explained as a way to keep the ink’s flavor from being masked by salty cheese.
