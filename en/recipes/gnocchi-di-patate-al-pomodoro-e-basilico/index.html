Gnocchi di Patate al Pomodoro e BasilicoPotato Gnocchi with Tomato and Basil

Difficulty★★★☆☆ Prep55 min Cook1 hr 48 min servings4

Traditional gnocchi made by boiling (or steaming) whole, unpeeled floury potatoes, passing them through a sieve while hot to release moisture, and bringing them together with as little flour as possible. More flour makes the dough easier to handle but also makes it heavy and dumpling-like, so judging the amount from the condition of the potatoes is essential. The sauce is a light pomodoro of canned whole tomatoes briefly cooked with garlic and olive oil, with basil added just before turning off the heat to release its fragrance.

IngredientsMethodAbout this dish

Ingredients (4 servings)

  • Floury potatoes, such as russets (not new potatoes)1000 gChoose similar sizes. Wash and cook whole without peeling.
  • Soft wheat flour (Italian type 00)300 gStart with 200g and adjust to a maximum of 300g according to the dough. Do not use for dusting.
  • Egg1 eggMedium. Beat just before using; binds the dough.
  • Salt for the dough8 gSprinkle directly onto the sieved potatoes.
  • Nutmeg0.5 gGrate fresh from a whole nutmeg.
  • Semolina for dusting and trays50 gSprinkle over the work surface and trays for the shaped gnocchi to prevent sticking.
  • Coarse salt for the cooking water60 gAdd at 10g/L for 6L of water.
  • Canned whole tomatoes800 gCrush by hand and remove tough cores and stem ends. Use the juices too.
  • Extra virgin olive oil60 ml45ml for cooking the sauce and 15ml for drizzling at the end.
  • Garlic2 clovesPeel, lightly crush with the flat of a knife and remove the germ. Infuse whole, then remove.
  • Fresh basil30 gSubmerge 1 sprig with its stem in the sauce to infuse it, add 10 leaves just before tossing and reserve 8 small leaves for garnish.
  • Salt for the sauce5 gAdd in separate portions during cooking and at the end.
  • Granulated sugar2 gFor balancing particularly acidic tomatoes.
  • Black peppercorns1 gGrind fresh over the finished dish.
  • Grated Parmigiano Reggiano60 g40g for tossing and 20g at the table.

Method

140 min

Cook the potatoes in their skins

  • 1 Meanwhile(While the potatoes cook) Make the sauce in step 4. Once the sauce is ready, use that burner to boil 1L water in a small pot and keep it ready for the test cook.
  • 2 Wash 1000g floury potatoes and remove any sprouts. Put them whole and unpeeled in a pot, cover with water without adding salt, and place over high heat.
  • 3 Once boiling, reduce to medium heat so the surface moves gently. Cook for 30–40 minutes, lifting out each potato as soon as a skewer passes through it.

Ready when

The potatoes are done when a skewer passes through the center without resistance and the potato does not cling to it as you pull it out.

Caution

Peeled potatoes absorb water while boiling, forcing you to add more flour and making heavy gnocchi. Always cook them whole in their skins.

A rolling boil splits the skins and lets water inside. Keep the surface moving gently.

220 min

Sieve while hot and make the dough

  • 1 Hold the hot potatoes with a cloth, peel them, and pass them through a sieve onto the work surface, spreading them out. Leave for 5 minutes to release steam.
  • 2 When cool enough to touch, sprinkle with 8g salt and 0.3g freshly grated nutmeg. Pour over 1 egg beaten just before use and sift in 200g flour.
  • 3 Cut and combine with a dough scraper. Once no dry flour remains, press 3–4 times with the heel of your hand to bring together. If sticky, add flour in 20g increments, up to 300g in total.

Ready when

The dough is ready when it barely sticks to your hands, is soft enough to retain a finger impression, and does not tear under its own weight when lifted.

Caution

A masher or food processor breaks the cells and makes the potatoes sticky. Use a sieve or potato ricer.

Overkneading develops gluten and makes the gnocchi hard and heavy. Press as little as possible.

310 min

Check the flour with a test cook (prova di cottura)

  • 1 Meanwhile(During the test cook) Cover the dough with a cloth to prevent drying.
  • 2 Pinch off 2 thumb-tip-sized pieces of dough, roll into balls, and drop into boiling water.
  • 3 Use 30 seconds after they float as a guide, then check the center with a food thermometer and continue cooking for 1 minute after it reaches at least 75°C. Lift out, cut open, and check the interior and texture.
  • 4 If they fall apart or the cut surface is sticky, add 20g flour and test again, up to 300g in total. If they still fall apart at this limit, discard the dough, get a fresh batch of stored floury potatoes and the other ingredients, and start again from step 1. If they taste floury, do not add flour: cook another piece for longer and check again. If they still taste floury after thorough cooking, or are too hard, discard the dough and start again from step 1.

Ready when

The flour amount is right when a test piece floats without losing its shape, has an evenly moist interior when cut, and leaves no floury taste when chewed.

Caution

Skipping this test and shaping the whole batch risks having all the dough dissolve in the water if there is too little flour, or making the whole batch heavy and hard if there is too much. You cannot correct this afterward.

Use a separate small pot of water for the test: water in which dough has dissolved will make the main batch cloudy.

Tips

Because potato moisture varies, local cooks always test 1–2 pieces before shaping the whole batch. This test piece, rather than the recipe numbers, guides the decision.

435 min

Make a light pomodoro sauce

  • 1 Crush 800g canned whole tomatoes by hand in a bowl, including their juices, and remove any tough cores and stem ends.
  • 2 Put 45ml olive oil and 2 peeled, crushed garlic cloves with the germs removed in a frying pan. Cook over low heat for 5–6 minutes and remove the garlic when lightly colored.
  • 3 Add the tomatoes and raise to medium heat. Once simmering, add 3g salt and cook for 20 minutes, occasionally scraping the bottom.
  • 4 Submerge 1 basil sprig, cook over low heat for 5 minutes, and remove it. If the acidity is sharp, dissolve in 2g granulated sugar. Season with 2g salt and turn off the heat.
  • 5 Set aside half the sauce in another container.

Ready when

The sauce is ready when a wooden spatula drawn across the bottom leaves a track for 2–3 seconds and the oil and tomato remain combined.

Caution

Garlic burns and becomes bitter over high heat. Infuse it slowly over low heat.

Overreducing makes the sauce too heavy and concentrated for the gnocchi. Stop while it still flows lightly.

530 min

Shape the gnocchi

  • 1 Meanwhile(While shaping) Bring 6L water to a boil in a large pot.
  • 2 Divide the dough into 6 portions and cover with a cloth. Lightly dust the work surface with 20g semolina. Roll 1 portion at a time into a rope 2cm thick and cut into 2cm lengths.
  • 3 Gently press and roll each piece with your thumb over the back of a fork or a gnocchi board to make grooves. Arrange on 2 trays dusted with 30g semolina.

Ready when

Shaping is complete when all the gnocchi have grooves and sit separately on the trays without sticking together.

Caution

Too much dusting makes the surface floury and prevents sauce from clinging. Use just enough to leave a thin white coating on the work surface.

If left too long after shaping, the potatoes release moisture and stick to the surface. Once all are shaped, begin cooking immediately, starting with those shaped first.

618 min

Cook the gnocchi

  • 1 Meanwhile(Before cooking) Warm 4 plates with hot water and dry them.
  • 2 Dissolve 60g coarse salt in 6L boiling water and adjust the heat so the surface moves gently.
  • 3 Repeat steps 6–8 in 2 batches, using half the gnocchi each time. Gently cook half the gnocchi; use 20–30 seconds after they float as a guide, then measure the center of the largest piece with a food thermometer. Once the center has remained at or above 75°C for 1 minute, lift them out and transfer to the frying pan containing half the sauce.

Ready when

The gnocchi should retain their shape and their centers must have remained at or above 75°C for 1 minute.

Caution

If half the gnocchi overlap in the pot, divide into smaller batches. Divide the sauce and finishing ingredients in steps 7–8 in the same proportions. Coat and plate each batch as soon as it is cooked.

Tips

Cooking time varies with gnocchi size, so rising to the surface is a more useful guide than the clock.

75 min

Toss with the sauce and finish

  • 1 Gently shake the pan over medium heat to combine half the sauce with half the gnocchi. If too thick, add a little cooking water as needed.
  • 2 Turn off the heat, add 20g Parmigiano and 5 torn basil leaves, and gently shake to combine.

Ready when

The dish is ready when sauce clings to the grooves and makes the gnocchi glossy, with no watery sauce pooling in the pan.

Caution

Stirring with a wooden spatula breaks the gnocchi. Combine them by gently shaking the pan.

Adding cheese over the heat makes it clump and become stringy. Turn off the heat first.

Tips

Starch in the cooking water binds the sauce and oil into a coating on the gnocchi.

85 min

Plate

  • 1 Divide the finished half-batch between 2 warmed plates, distributing the sauce evenly.
  • 2 Drizzle each plate with 3.75ml olive oil (15ml across 4 plates). Divide the full 1g black pepper among the plates, freshly grinding it over each, and garnish with 2 basil leaves per plate.

Ready when

The dish is complete when each plate has glossy sauce, pepper, and basil.

Caution

Cold plates cool the gnocchi rapidly and firm their surface. Use warmed plates.

Tips

Serve the remaining 20g Parmigiano in a small dish at the table for everyone to add themselves, as is customary locally.

About this dish

Potato gnocchi are made throughout Italy, but the version dressed with a light tomato and basil sauce belongs to Campania, centered on Naples.

Potatoes became established on Italian tables from the 18th century onward; earlier gnocchi were made from bread or semolina. Potatoes lightened the dough, and in the south it became paired with summer tomatoes. Bake the same dough in an earthenware dish with mozzarella and it becomes gnocchi alla sorrentina.

Some areas around Naples still keep the household tradition of making gnocchi on Thursdays, and traces of it remain in trattoria daily specials. In midsummer, when tomatoes are sweetest, the sauce is cooked for even less time.

Chef Authen’s tip

Locally, cooks always test just 1–2 pieces in boiling water after making the dough. This step, called prova di cottura, settles the only difficult judgment in potato gnocchi. Potato moisture changes every time, so the amount of flour is determined by this 1 test piece, rather than the numbers in a recipe.
