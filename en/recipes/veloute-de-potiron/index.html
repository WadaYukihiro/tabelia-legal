Velouté de PotironClassic Pumpkin Velouté

Difficulty★★★☆☆ Prep15 min Cook1 hr 19 min servings6

A classical velouté of pumpkin simmered in chicken stock lightly thickened with a blond roux, then passed through a fine sieve until silky. A liaison of egg yolks and cream binds the soup, and cold butter adds gloss and roundness. The three stages of roux, straining and liaison create the texture described as velvety, rather than simply mashing the vegetables into a potage. Following the classical potage velouté form, buttery croutons are served separately.

Method

115 min

Prepare the pumpkin, aromatics and measured ingredients

  • 1 Remove the pumpkin’s skin, seeds and fibres and cut a net 700 g into even 3 cm cubes.
  • 2 Halve 120 g white Japanese long onion lengthwise, rinse between its layers under running water and slice across at 5 mm intervals.
  • 3 Slice 80 g onion across the grain to 2 mm thick.
  • 4 Tie 2 thyme sprigs and 1 bay leaf with kitchen string into a bouquet garni.
  • 5 Divide 150 ml thick crème fraîche into 120 ml in a bowl and 30 ml in a small dish.
  • 6 Divide 60 g unsalted butter into 25 g for sweating and the roux, 20 g for monter au beurre, the emulsifying finish with cold butter, and 15 g for croutons.
  • 7 Cut the 20 g finishing butter into 1.5 cm cubes, spread on a plate and refrigerate.
  • 8 Sift 20 g flour through a tea strainer onto a small plate.

Ready when

The pumpkin cubes should be even, with all aromatics, the bouquet garni, flour and dairy measured and ready.

212 min

Sweat the onions without colouring

  • 1 Melt the 25 g butter for sweating and the roux in a heavy pot over low heat until gently foaming.
  • 2 Add the long onion and onion, sprinkle with 2 g salt and coat with butter using a wooden spoon.
  • 3 Lay baking paper over the vegetables, fitting it against the sides of the pot, and sweat over low heat for 10–12 minutes, stirring every 2 minutes.

Ready when

The onions should be translucent and softened, with no browning on either the vegetables or the pot.

The sharp raw onion smell should have become sweet.

36 min

Make a blond roux and add the stock

  • 1 Keeping the heat low, sprinkle 20 g sifted flour over the pot and stir with a wooden spoon until no dry flour remains.
  • 2 Cook for 2–3 minutes over low heat, stirring constantly, until the roux is blond, a pale straw colour.
  • 3 First pour in 200 ml of the 700 ml chicken stock, whisking briskly until smooth.
  • 4 Add the remaining 500 ml stock in 2 additions, whisking until even each time.

Ready when

The raw flour smell should have become lightly nutty, and the liquid should be evenly thickened without lumps.

432 min

Simmer the pumpkin and fry the croutons

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile it simmers, remove the crusts from 2 bread slices and cut into 1 cm cubes. Melt the 15 g crouton butter in a frying pan over medium heat, fry the cubes for 3–4 minutes, shaking constantly until golden, and transfer to kitchen paper.
  • 2 Add 700 g diced pumpkin and the bouquet garni to the pot and stir once with a wooden spoon.
  • 3 Bring to a boil over medium heat in about 5 minutes, then carefully skim the surface with a ladle for about 2 minutes.
  • 4 Cover with the lid slightly ajar and simmer over low heat for 25 minutes, stirring from the bottom every 5 minutes.

Ready when

A skewer should slide through the pumpkin without resistance, and the cooking liquid should lightly coat the wooden spoon.

510 min

Strain for a velvety texture

  • 1 Remove the bouquet garni with tongs and discard.
  • 2 Transfer the contents, about 1.5 L in total, to a blender in 2–3 batches and blend each on high for 1 minute until smooth.
  • 3 Pour the purée into a chinois, a fine conical sieve, and press through into another pot with a wooden spoon.
  • 4 Scrape out and discard the fibres left inside the sieve, and scrape the purée from its outside into the pot.

Ready when

Only fibres should remain in the sieve; the velouté should be silky with an even sheen.

Caution

Hot liquid can erupt from a blender. Follow its temperature and capacity limits.

68 min

Bind with the egg-yolk and cream liaison

  • 1 Whisk 3 egg yolks and the reserved 120 ml crème fraîche in a bowl until uniform to make the liaison, an egg-and-cream thickener.
  • 2 Warm the strained velouté over low heat until just before small bubbles form at the edge; do not boil.
  • 3 Gradually whisk 3 ladles of hot velouté into the liaison, 1 ladle at a time, to equalise the temperature.
  • 4 Return the warmed liaison to the pot and cook over low heat for 3–4 minutes, maintaining a thin wisp of steam and stirring constantly with a wooden spoon in a figure of 8.

Ready when

It should coat the back of the spoon and hold a line drawn through it with a finger, the nappé stage.

There should be no bubbles at the sides, only a steady light steam.

Caution

Boiling after adding the yolks will make them coagulate and separate.

75 min

Mount with butter and season with nutmeg

  • 1 Turn off the heat and add the chilled 20 g butter in 3 additions, gently swirling the pot and stirring with a wooden spoon to melt it in.
  • 2 Add the remaining 4 g salt in 2 g additions, tasting as you go, then grind in 1/2 tsp white pepper.
  • 3 Grate in 1/4 tsp nutmeg and stir gently.

Ready when

The surface should have a mirror-like sheen, with balanced salt and nutmeg and a rounded flavour.

86 min

Marble with cream and serve croutons separately

  • 1 Pour boiling water into 6 deep plates and leave for 2 minutes, then discard the water and dry with a cloth.
  • 2 Divide the velouté evenly among the warm plates and turn them gently to level the surface.
  • 3 Divide the reserved 30 ml crème fraîche into dots over the plates and draw swirls with a skewer tip to create marbling.
  • 4 Place the croutons in a small bowl and serve alongside.

Ready when

White marbling should stand out against the orange soup in the warm plates, with the croutons served separately.

About this dish

In Paris’s markets, large pumpkins begin to pile up in October and are sold in cut portions. From autumn into winter, pumpkin soup has long been enjoyed in France as the first course of a family supper.

The name velouté comes from velours, the French word for velvet. In classical French cuisine, systematised from the 19th to the early 20th century, soups were divided into clear and thickened types. Veloutés were defined as a category of thickened soup finished with egg yolks and cream. This classification also appears in Escoffier’s cookbook.

Rather than belonging to one particular region, it is treated in restaurants and homes alike as an autumn starter made with seasonal pumpkin. The classical serving style places butter-fried croutons alongside in a separate dish.

Chef Authen’s tip

French broadly distinguishes two kinds of pumpkin. Potiron denotes winter squash, while citrouille, also used for Halloween lanterns, belongs to a different botanical species. In Perrault’s Cinderella, it is a citrouille that becomes a carriage. Japanese kabocha belongs on the potiron side. Citrouille for magic and potiron for soup: their roles are clearly divided.
