Moules MarinièresMussels Marinière

Difficulty★★☆☆☆ Prep17 min Cook14 min servings4

Sailor-style mussels steamed in white wine, enjoyed in autumn and winter along the coast of northern France, including Brittany. The cleaned mussels are simply steamed quickly with white wine, shallots and butter, but meticulous cleaning and judging the exact moment to stop cooking determine everything. The traditional way to eat them is to use an empty shell as tongs to remove the flesh, then soak up every last drop of broth with baguette.

Method

112 min

Debeard the mussels

  • 1 Prepare the mussels.
  • 2 Wash in a bowl of water, rubbing the shells against each other.
  • 3 Scrub away barnacles and dirt on the outsides of the shells with a brush.
  • 4 Pull the byssal threads, or beards, emerging between the shells toward the hinge to remove them.
  • 5 Discard mussels that remain open even when tapped lightly, and those with cracked shells.
  • 6 Removing the beard weakens the mussel, so do this immediately before cooking.

Ready when

Proceed once all beards have been removed and the shell surfaces are clean.

25 min

Chop the aromatics and slice the baguette

  • 1 Finely chop 3 shallots.
  • 2 Finely chop 1 garlic clove.
  • 3 Roughly chop the flat-leaf parsley.
  • 4 Slice the baguette 2–3 cm thick.
34 min

Sauté the shallots in butter

  • 1 Melt 40 g butter over medium heat in a large pot with a lid, add the chopped shallots and garlic and sauté for 2–3 minutes.
  • 2 Stop sautéing when translucent and sweet-smelling, before they take on color.
  • 3 Add 2 thyme sprigs and 1 bay leaf.
47 min

Steam the mussels in white wine

  • 1 Increase to high heat, pour in 300 ml white wine and boil for about 1 minute to remove the smell of alcohol.
  • 2 Add all the mussels at once, cover and steam over high heat for 4–5 minutes.
  • 3 During cooking, hold the pot with both hands and shake it vigorously up and down 1–2 times to redistribute the mussels.
  • 4 Turn off the heat as soon as the shells open.
  • 5 Continued cooking makes the flesh shrink and toughen, so finish when most have opened.

Ready when

Discard any mussels that remain closed.

52 min

Stir in parsley and plate

  • 1 Grind black pepper into the pot, add the roughly chopped parsley and mix with broad movements, lifting from the bottom.
  • 2 Once the parsley is evenly distributed, serve the mussels with their broth in deep plates.
61 min

Serve with baguette

  • 1 Serve with lightly warmed baguette.
  • 2 Traditionally, an empty mussel shell is used like tongs to remove the flesh from the next shell, and the remaining broth is enjoyed to the last drop by dipping in baguette.
  • 3 The broth is an integral part of the dish.

About this dish

Along the northern French coast from Brittany to Normandy, mussels have long been farmed on wooden stakes called bouchots driven into tidal flats. Exposed to the ebb and flow of the tides, the mussels develop firm flesh and are an indispensable seafood at the local table.

Mussels are in season from early autumn into winter, when cooler water helps them become plump. In September, black pots begin appearing in the bistros and brasseries of port towns, served with heaped fries as moules-frites. The pot is brought straight to the table, and diners traditionally use an empty shell as tongs as they eat.

As the name marinière, or sailor-style, suggests, this began as a rustic dish of mussels steamed by sailors and fishermen with whatever white wine and aromatic vegetables were at hand. Today it is a brasserie staple throughout France, welcomed as a herald of autumn.

Chef Authen’s tip

Mussels raised on bouchot stakes grow without touching the sandy seabed and therefore contain very little sand. The moules de bouchot of Mont-Saint-Michel Bay became the first shellfish granted French AOP protected designation of origin, showing how the farming method itself guarantees quality. A mussel is at its plumpest the instant its shell opens. An extra 5 minutes “just in case” is not insurance, merely overcooking. Staying beside the pot is the only etiquette this dish requires.
