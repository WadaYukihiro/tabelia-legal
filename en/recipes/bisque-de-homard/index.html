Bisque de HomardClassic Lobster Bisque Thickened with Rice

A classical lobster bisque thickened with rice. Lobster is sautéed and simmered in its shell, then crushed with cooked rice and strained thoroughly to extract its flavor. Finish with butter and cream without boiling.

Method

15 min

Check and weigh the chilled raw lobster pieces

  • 1 Check that the fishmonger has supplied 2 freshly split raw lobsters, each weighing about 450–500 g before splitting, with all head halves, claws, legs and shells retained. Keep chilled and prepare the same day.

Ready when

Continue when the uncooked chilled pieces from both lobsters are present and total about 1 kg.

220 min

Cut the lobster in its shell and remove stomach sacs and intestinal veins

  • 1 Separate the head halves from the tail halves at their join.
  • 2 Remove the stomach sac behind the eyes. Remove and discard all tomalley, the soft green material in the body cavity; do not add it to the bisque.
  • 3 Remove the exposed intestinal vein from the split tail, then cut each tail half crosswise into 4 shell-on sections.
  • 4 Remove the claws at their bases and crack the shells with the back of a knife.
  • 5 Cut 50 g carrot and 50 g onion into brunoise, very fine dice.

Ready when

Continue when each lobster is divided into head halves, tail sections and claws, with stomach sacs, intestinal veins and all tomalley removed.

Caution

Leaving the stomach sac gives the bisque a sandy texture and bitterness.

310 min

Sauté aromatics and lobster in butter until the shells turn red

  • 1 Melt the 40g cooking butter in a large heavy pot over medium heat. Add carrot, onion, 1 thyme sprig, 1/2 bay leaf and 3 parsley stems and cook for 3–4 minutes without browning.
  • 2 Add the lobster, increase to medium-high heat and cook for 5–6 minutes, turning with a wooden spatula.
  • 3 Sprinkle with 12g salt and 0.5g white pepper and mix.

Ready when

Continue when all the shells are bright red and fragrant.

Caution

Scorched shells give the bisque a burnt bitterness.

415 min

Reduce Cognac and wine and simmer in broth for 10 minutes

  • 1 Pour in 5ml Cognac and 200ml white wine. Boil over medium heat for 3–4 minutes to reduce by half.
  • 2 Add 250ml chicken broth, cover and simmer over medium-low heat for 10 minutes.
  • 3 MeanwhileWhile reducing, put 180g rice and 750ml chicken broth in another saucepan over medium heat. Once boiling, reduce to low heat, cover and cook for 20 minutes.

Ready when

The wine is sufficiently reduced when its volume has halved.

Continue after 10 minutes of simmering, when the tail meat lifts away from its shell and turns white and firm.

510 min

Reserve and dice tail meat for the garnish

  • 1 Remove the tail rounds and claws from the pot and take the meat out of the shells.
  • 2 Cut half the tail meat into 1cm cubes and reserve for garnish; return all remaining meat and removed shells to the pot.

Ready when

Continue when the garnish is set aside and all remaining meat and shells are back in the pot.

620 min

Crush shells and rice, strain, dilute with broth and strain again

  • 1 Remove the thyme, bay leaf and parsley stems.
  • 2 Lift the hard claw and head shells from the pot, put them in a sturdy plastic bag and break them with a rolling pin.
  • 3 Combine all the crushed shells from the bag, the other shells and legs, all remaining lobster meat and the cooked aromatics from the pot with the cooked rice and only 2 ladlefuls of cooking liquid. Process in 3 batches, filling the food processor no more than halfway, until the shells are finely crushed.
  • 4 Combine with the remaining liquid in the pot, transfer to a chinois, a fine conical strainer, and press firmly with the bottom of a ladle into another pot.
  • 5 Pour the remaining 500ml chicken broth over the shell residue as if rinsing it, then add to the pot and stir.
  • 6 Bring briefly to a boil over medium heat and strain again through the chinois.

Ready when

Continue when the bisque is smooth and lightly coats the back of a spoon.

Caution

Processing too much hot liquid can make it erupt through gaps in the lid and cause burns.

Tips

Pressing the shells and legs thoroughly extracts their flavor into the bisque.

78 min

Finish with butter, cream and cayenne

  • 1 Warm the bisque over low heat and take off the heat just before boiling.
  • 2 Add the 150g cold finishing butter in several additions, whisking until incorporated.
  • 3 Mix in 100ml cream and 0.2g cayenne pepper and taste.
  • 4 Add the reserved meat to the bisque, warm for 1 minute and pour into warmed bowls.

Ready when

The bisque is ready when the butter is incorporated, the surface is glossy and no oil floats on top.

Caution

Boiling after adding butter and cream makes the soup separate, leaving fat on top.

About this dish

In 17th-century France, bisque meant a squab soup. The 1652 edition of La Varenne’s cookbook placed squab bisque first among its soups, decorating the broth-cooked birds with cockscombs and pistachios. It was also served on Saint Louis’s Day, August 25, 1690, when the minister Louvois hosted Louis XIV.

The custom of calling chicken or squab soups bisque lasted until the end of the 18th century. The name later came to mean a purée of crustaceans such as crayfish and lobster. Its origin is uncertain: explanations connect it either with the Bay of Biscay or with a term meaning “cooked twice.”

The Breton coast once had plentiful lobster, but numbers are said to have declined by the end of the 20th century. France now lands about 500–700 metric tons annually. In Brittany, lobsters with a carapace under 87mm may not be caught, and landing claws alone is also forbidden.

Chef Authen’s tip

Furetière’s dictionary, published in 1690, is said to have described bisque as a fine soup served only at the tables of great nobles. Over more than 300 years, squab gave way to marine crustaceans, while bisque remained a luxurious soup requiring considerable work.
