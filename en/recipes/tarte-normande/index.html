Tarte NormandeNormandy Apple Tart

Difficulty★★★★☆ Prep1 hr 46 min Cook1 hr 15 min Rest4 hr servings6

A classic baked tart combining Normandy apples, crème fraîche and Calvados. The pâte brisée, made with just 10g sugar, is blind-baked, brushed with egg white and baked again to seal it. A rich almond custard is then poured around radially arranged apples and baked gently until set. Key techniques include leaving cold butter pieces in the dough, fitting it snugly into the corners and preventing puffing and shrinkage during blind baking, and judging the heat to balance apple moisture with the setting of the custard. The care preserves a crisp base and clearly defined apples. Bake in a 22cm pan, cool completely to room temperature and dust with icing sugar just before serving.

Method

125 min

Make pâte brisée with visible cold butter pieces

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile the butter chills in the freezer, wipe the worktop and rolling pin and set aside 10g dusting flour in a small bowl.
  • 2 Cut 70g unsalted pastry butter into 1.5cm cubes, spread on a tray and chill in the freezer for 15 minutes.
  • 3 Put 130g cake flour, 10g pastry sugar and 2g salt in a bowl, whisk for 30 seconds and refrigerate the bowl.
  • 4 Prepare ice water, measure 20ml cold water from it, beat with 1 egg yolk and return to the refrigerator; cover and refrigerate the egg white separately.
  • 5 Add the frozen butter to the chilled flour and cut it through the flour with a dough scraper to form coarse pieces about 5mm across.
  • 6 Quickly rub the butter into the flour with your fingertips until it resembles coarse breadcrumbs.
  • 7 Pour in the egg-yolk and water mixture in 3 additions, cutting it in with the scraper each time until no dry flour remains.
  • 8 Turn onto the worktop and press the dough away from you with the heel of your hand only 2–3 times, using fraisage, to bring it together. Shape into a 2cm-thick disc and wrap.

Ready when

The dough has visible marbled streaks of butter and holds together without crumbling when squeezed.

Caution

Overkneading makes the pastry more likely to shrink during baking.

21 hr

Rest the dough for 1 hour to relax the gluten

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile the dough rests, wipe the inside of a 22cm loose-bottomed tart pan with a dry cloth, cut 1 sheet of baking parchment larger than the pan, and measure out baking weights.
  • 2 Place the wrapped dough on the coldest refrigerator shelf and rest for 1 hour.

Ready when

The dough is evenly firm and a finger pressed into it leaves a slow-forming mark.

320 min

Roll and fit snugly into a 22cm pan

  • 1 On a floured worktop, roll the dough into a circle 30cm in diameter and 3mm thick.
  • 2 Loosely wrap it around the rolling pin, transfer it over the 22cm tart pan and unfold from the center outward.
  • 3 Ease the dough into the bottom corners and press the sides against the pan with the pads of your fingers so it fits snugly into every corner.
  • 4 Roll the rolling pin across the rim to trim off excess pastry.
  • 5 Prick the entire base with a fork at 2cm intervals.

Ready when

There are no air pockets at the bottom corners and the pastry fits evenly against the pan all the way to the rim.

Caution

Stretching the pastry forcibly makes it more likely to shrink in the oven.

Tips

If the dough is hard and cracks, leave at room temperature for 3–5 minutes; if too soft, refrigerate for 5 minutes.

430 min

Chill the lined pan to prevent shrinkage

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile it chills, preheat the oven to 180°C.
  • 2 Place the lined pan on a baking tray and refrigerate uncovered for 30 minutes.

Ready when

The surface is dry and firm and does not dent softly when touched.

530 min

Blind-bake a firm shell that holds its shape

  • 1 Line the chilled pastry with baking parchment and pack with baking weights right into the corners without gaps.
  • 2 Bake on the middle rack at 180°C for 20 minutes.
  • 3 Remove the weights and paper, bake at 180°C for another 8 minutes and remove when the base is pale golden.

Ready when

No white, underbaked areas remain on the base; the shell is uniformly pale golden.

The sides have not pulled away from the pan and retain their height.

66 min

Seal the base with baked egg white

  • 1 Lightly loosen the reserved egg white with a fork and brush 10g in a thin layer over the inside base and sides of the shell immediately after blind baking, while still hot.
  • 2 Return to the oven at 180°C for 3 minutes; when the egg white has dried into a thin film, remove and place on a rack.
  • 3 Reduce the oven setting to 170°C.

Ready when

The entire base has a thin, dry, slightly shiny film and is not sticky to the touch.

725 min

Sauté the apples in 3 batches and coat with Calvados

  • 1 Peel and core the apples to obtain 400g prepared fruit, then cut into even 5mm-thick slices.
  • 2 Melt 5g of the unsalted butter for the apples in a frying pan about 26cm across over medium heat. When the bubbles become fine, arrange 1/3 of the apples in a single layer without overlaps.
  • 3 Cook over medium heat for 2 minutes on one side, turn gently with a spatula and cook for another 2 minutes. Once lightly colored, spread in a single layer on a tray.
  • 4 Repeat with the remaining 2 batches, melting 5g butter each time, arranging the apples in a single layer, cooking for 2 minutes per side and spreading on the tray.
  • 5 Return all 3 batches to the pan, turn off the heat and drizzle 10ml Calvados over them.
  • 6 Return to medium heat and boil for 30–40 seconds while shaking the pan to drive off the alcohol smell.
  • 7 Spread the apples on a tray without overlaps. Discard any juices left in the pan rather than adding them.

Ready when

The apples hold their shape without rounded edges and are lightly browned on the surface.

Almost no liquid remains in the pan and the sharp alcohol smell has disappeared.

Caution

Do not ignite the Calvados.

815 min

Make a smooth almond custard

  • 1 MeanwhileWhile making the custard, place the tray of apples in a breezy spot to cool.
  • 2 Whisk 1 whole egg with 50g custard sugar in a bowl for 1 minute until pale.
  • 3 Split 1/2 vanilla pod lengthwise, scrape out the seeds with the back of a knife and add to the bowl.
  • 4 Add 100ml thick crème fraîche and 1g salt and stir slowly 20 times without whipping in air.
  • 5 Sift in 40g ground almonds and mix with a rubber spatula until no dry powder remains.
  • 6 Add 20ml Calvados, stir 10 times and pass through a fine strainer into another bowl.

Ready when

No lumps of powder or white egg streaks remain, and the custard flows evenly with a thick consistency.

The apples feel only lukewarm to the touch and no longer steam.

Caution

Incorporating too much air causes substantial sinking after baking.

910 min

Arrange the apples radially and fill to 3mm below the rim

  • 1 Arrange the cooled apple slices radially from the center outward in the sealed tart shell, slightly overlapping without gaps.
  • 2 Slowly pour the custard between the apples until it reaches 3mm below the pan rim.
  • 3 Place the pan on a baking tray and gently shake to level the custard.

Ready when

The custard reaches between the apples and is level at 3mm below the rim.

1045 min

Gently set the custard at 170°C

  • 1 Place the tray on the middle rack of the oven at 170°C and bake for 42–45 minutes.
  • 2 When baked, remove the pan and place on a rack.

Ready when

The entire surface is pale golden, and when gently shaken the center wobbles as one mass rather than rippling.

A skewer inserted in the center emerges without liquid custard.

112 hr 30 min

Cool at room temperature for 2 and a half hours

  • 1 Leave the tart in its pan on the rack at room temperature, away from direct sunlight and drafts, for 2 hours 30 minutes until completely cool.

Ready when

The pan base no longer feels warm, and the center does not wobble when the pan is lifted.

125 min

Unmold, dust and serve

  • 1 Lower and remove the sides of the loose-bottomed pan, slide a palette knife between the tart and metal base and transfer the tart to a plate.
  • 2 Just before serving, lightly sift 5g icing sugar through a tea strainer over the entire surface.

Ready when

The surface is evenly dusted white, with the contours of the apples visible through the sugar.

About this dish

Normandy, which includes Caen, the administrative center of Calvados, is a land of apples, butter and cream. Its rainy climate nourishes the grass, and tall apple trees stand in the pastures.

The apple harvest runs from September through November. Alongside their use for cider and Calvados, tart varieties are baked into desserts, and tarte normande has long been made in homes during the harvest season.

What distinguishes it is that all its principal ingredients apart from the pastry—apples, crème fraîche and Calvados—come from this region. It remains a staple in Normandy’s bakeries and pastry shops and is sliced after meals at weekend family tables.

Chef Authen’s tip

Normandy has fields called prés-vergers, combining pasture and orchard. Cows graze beneath tall apple trees; fallen apples become cider, while the cows’ milk becomes cream and butter. The apples and cream in tarte normande originally came from the same field. A single pasture is gathered into one dessert.
